From DMRG to a Certified Ground State: A Workflow for Stetcu–Baroni Filtering of Matrix-Product Trial States, with Its Accuracy and Gate Cost

Draft — authors to be added

8 October 2026

Abstract

Density-matrix renormalisation group (DMRG) gives a matrix-product state and the low-lying energies of a spin chain classically, but a compact trial circuit built from them keeps some excited-state weight. The projection filter of Stetcu, Baroni and Carlson removes it on a quantum computer using one ancilla, controlled time evolution and post-selection. We give a complete workflow around this filter, from the DMRG front end to a verified state, and evaluate it on the open J1J_1–J2J_2 spin-½ chain for N=4N=4–1616 with real DMRG trial states compiled to circuits. The paper is organised around three questions. (1) Can the filter be certified without knowing the exact ground state? Yes, conditionally: an a-priori bound on the trace distance to the ground state, D≤ℓ‾+ϵT/pgD\le\sqrt{\bar\ell}+\epsilon_T/\sqrt{p_g}, needs only DMRG-derived inputs and held on all 97 simulated points, but it fails if the ground-state weight γ\gamma or the gap Δ\Delta is over-estimated (a gap 25%25\% too large already breaks it). (2) How conservative is the certificate? Its leakage term is reasonably tight (1.1–2.7×\times); its worst-case Trotter term is extremely conservative (9–77×\times, growing with NN), so the certified step count is 11–168×\times what is empirically sufficient. (3) Can the practical cost be recovered without the exact ground state? A hybrid rule, certified leakage plus a measured nn-versus-2n2n Trotter estimate, met every target (25 of 25) with 6–66×\times fewer steps than the certificate, but it is empirical, not rigorous. In gates, the hybrid filter costs 3×1023\times10^{2}–4×1044\times10^{4} CX including the trial circuit, growing as roughly N2.5N^{2.5}–N3.1N^{3.1}, which is 28–151×\times the cost of preparing the state directly from the MPS at the same fidelity. The workflow is a certified, reproducible way to use the filter and a calibrated account of its cost, not a resource advantage on these chains.

1. Introduction

A standard hybrid route to a ground state is to run DMRG, compile the resulting matrix-product state (MPS) into a shallow circuit, and repair the circuit’s residual error with a quantum projection step. The Stetcu–Baroni–Carlson (SBC) projection [1], related to the rodeo algorithm [2], multiplies the trial state’s energy components by a filter F(E)=∏icos(Eti+ϕi)F(E)=\prod_i\cos(Et_i+\phi_i) realised with pulses of controlled time evolution on a single ancilla. It sits among a family of ground-state projectors that use polynomial or Fourier filters of the Hamiltonian: phase-estimation-based preparation [3], quantum-eigenvalue-transformation filters [4,5] and, more generally, quantum singular value transformation. The SBC filter trades the optimal log(1/ε)\log(1/\varepsilon) scaling of those methods for a measurement-based, single-ancilla circuit whose filter can be designed and certified classically.

The practical difficulty is that the guarantee one would like, a bound on how far the prepared state is from the ground state, seems to need the ground state. This paper builds a workflow that avoids that, and asks three questions about it:

Question Short answer Where
Q1 Can SBC filtering be certified without knowing the exact ground state? Yes, conditionally on conservative bounds for the ground-state weight γ\gamma and the gap Δ\Delta. A certificate built from DMRG-derived inputs was never violated, and it fails if γ\gamma or Δ\Delta is over-estimated. Section 4
Q2 How conservative is that certificate? Leakage is reasonably tight; worst-case Trotter error is extremely conservative (and more so as NN grows). Section 5
Q3 Can we recover the practical cost without the exact ground state? Largely, with the nn-versus-2n2n hybrid rule, which met every target, but it is empirical rather than rigorous. Section 6

Section 8 states the limitations. We do not claim a resource advantage; Section 7 shows there is none at these sizes.

2. The workflow

Stage 1 — classical front end (DMRG). From a Hamiltonian H=∑gHgH=\sum_gH_g (here the bonds of the chain) run DMRG for the ground-state energy E0E_0 and a high-bond-dimension reference MPS, DMRG on −H-H for the top energy EtopE_{\mathrm{top}}, and DMRG on H+λ|ψ0⟩⟨ψ0|H+\lambda|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0| with λ=1.1(Etop−E0)\lambda=1.1(E_{\mathrm{top}}-E_0) for the first excitation E1E_1 (penalty method). Set W=Etop−E0W=E_{\mathrm{top}}-E_0, Hs=(H−E0)/WH_s=(H-E_0)/W, so that specHs⊂{0}∪[Δ,1]\operatorname{spec}H_s\subset\{0\}\cup[\Delta,1] with Δ=(E1−E0)/W\Delta=(E_1-E_0)/W.

Stage 2 — trial state. Run DMRG at a small maximum bond dimension χ\chi and compile the MPS to a circuit (here exactly, by sequential isometries with mps-to-circuit; an approximate brickwork compilation is also tested). Estimate the ground-state weight γ=|⟨E0|ψ⟩|2\gamma=|\langle E_0|\psi\rangle|^2 as the overlap with the reference MPS. If 1−γ≤ε1-\gamma\le\varepsilon for the target ε\varepsilon, stop: the trial state already suffices.

Stage 3 — filter. One pulse (t,ϕ)(t,\phi) applies 𝖧ae−itHs⊗ZaRza(2ϕ)𝖧a\mathsf H_{\mathrm{a}}\,e^{-itH_s\otimes Z_{\mathrm{a}}}\,\mathrm{Rz}_{\mathrm{a}}(2\phi)\,\mathsf H_{\mathrm{a}}; its ancilla-|0⟩|0\rangle block is cos(Hst+ϕ)\cos(H_st+\phi). Keeping outcome 00 after mm pulses applies F(E)=∏i=1mcos(Eti+ϕi)F(E)=\prod_{i=1}^m\cos(Et_i+\phi_i), with total time T=∑itiT=\sum_it_i and F(0)=∏icosϕiF(0)=\prod_i\cos\phi_i. Two numbers characterise the filter: η=supE∈[Δ,1]|F(E)|/|F(0)|\eta=\sup_{E\in[\Delta,1]}|F(E)|/|F(0)| (suppression of excited states relative to the ground state) and pg=γF(0)2p_g=\gamma F(0)^2 (a lower bound on the success probability). We design (ti,ϕi)(t_i,\phi_i) with floor.py, which maximises F(0)2F(0)^2 subject to a certified bound on η\eta (interval bisection using |F′|≤∑|ti||F'|\le\sum|t_i| and |F″|≤(∑|ti|)2|F''|\le(\sum|t_i|)^2), for a leakage budget εℓ=ε/4\varepsilon_\ell=\varepsilon/4.

Stage 4 — Trotterisation and the choice of nn. Each controlled evolution is kik_i first-order Lie–Trotter steps over the bonds, n=∑ikin=\sum_ik_i, ti=kidtt_i=k_i\,dt. The Trotter error is governed by the commutator constant α=∑g∥[Hg,∑g′>gHg′]∥\alpha=\sum_g\big\|[H_g,\sum_{g'>g}H_{g'}]\big\| (the larger of the two product orders; the ancilla does not change it). Three rules choose nn:

  • Certified. The smallest nn with D‾(n)≤ε\bar D(n)\le\sqrt\varepsilon, where the certificate is D≤D‾:=ℓ‾+αT22nγF(0)2,ℓ‾=(1−γ)η2γ+(1−γ)η2\boxed{\;D\le\bar D:=\sqrt{\bar\ell}+\frac{\alpha T^2}{2n\sqrt{\gamma F(0)^2}},\qquad\bar\ell=\frac{(1-\gamma)\eta^2}{\gamma+(1-\gamma)\eta^2}\;} DD is the trace distance between the implemented and the exact ground state. The first term is leakage (what an exact filter would leave) and depends only on γ\gamma and η\eta; the second is the Trotter term. Equivalently, infidelity ≤D‾2\le\bar D^2 and energy error ⟨H⟩−E0≤WD‾2\langle H\rangle-E_0\le W\bar D^2. The proof (Appendix A) is the triangle inequality for the trace distance plus a leakage lemma and a first-order Trotter lemma.
  • Hybrid. Keep the rigorous leakage term but replace the worst-case Trotter term by a measured one. For each candidate nn on a geometric grid, run the same filter at nn and 2n2n steps; because the first-order error scales as 1/n1/n, the Trotter distance is ≈2D(φ̃n,φ̃2n)\approx2D(\tilde\varphi_n,\tilde\varphi_{2n}). Accept the smallest nn such that ℓ‾+2D(φ̃n,φ̃2n)≤ε\sqrt{\bar\ell}+2D(\tilde\varphi_n,\tilde\varphi_{2n})\le\sqrt\varepsilon at that grid point and the next. This uses no ground state and is not a theorem (it assumes the 1/n1/n regime), but it is what a practitioner can actually evaluate.
  • Measured (oracle). The smallest nn whose measured DD meets the target. It needs the exact ground state, so it is a reference, not a rule.

Stage 5 — run and check. Execute the filter; check the post-selection rate, which is at least pgp_g, and, where the energy is measurable, ⟨H⟩−E0≤WD‾2\langle H\rangle-E_0\le W\bar D^2. If the device is noisy and prepares a mixed state ρ̃\tilde\rho, the triangle inequality adds a noise term: D(ρ̃,g)≤D(ρ̃,φ̃)+D‾D(\tilde\rho,g)\le D(\tilde\rho,\tilde\varphi)+\bar D.

3. Experiments

  • System. Open spin-½ J1J_1–J2J_2 chain, J1=1J_1=1, J2∈{0,0.4}J_2\in\{0,0.4\}, N∈{4,6,…,16}N\in\{4,6,\dots,16\}. Bonds are ordered: nearest-neighbour bonds with even then odd left site, then next-nearest-neighbour bonds likewise.
  • Front end. Two-site DMRG (quimb) with the repository’s get_energies.py; reference MPS up to bond dimension 64. The workflow sees only DMRG quantities. Exact diagonalisation is used afterwards to validate (true ground state, gap, overlap) and to measure distances.
  • Trial state. DMRG at maximum bond dimension χ=2\chi=2 (main experiments), compiled exactly. χ∈{1,2,4,8}\chi\in\{1,2,4,8\} and LL-layer approximate circuits are compared at N=8N=8.
  • Targets. ε=10−1,10−2\varepsilon=10^{-1},10^{-2} (N≤16N\le16) and 10−310^{-3} (N≤8N\le8), i.e. D≤0.32,0.1,0.032D\le0.32,0.1,0.032.
  • Simulation. Exact statevector simulation of the Trotterised filter, the ideal filter, and the ground state. A run is simulated only if n×bonds×2×2N≤1.5×1010n\times\text{bonds}\times2\times2^N\le1.5\times10^{10}; the N=16N=16, ε=10−2\varepsilon=10^{-2} certified circuits are not simulated, and their step counts and CX are reported from the certificate.
  • Cost. One Trotter step of one pulse (for each bond, the XX,YY,ZZXX,YY,ZZ rotations tensored with ZZ on the ancilla) is transpiled once with qiskit (level 3) to {CX,Rz,H,S}\{\mathrm{CX},\mathrm{Rz},\mathrm{H},\mathrm{S}\}; total CX is trial CX plus n×n\times CX per step. All-to-all connectivity unless stated. The trial circuit is the exact mps-to-circuit compilation, transpiled the same way.
  • Direct-preparation baseline. Preparing the state directly from the MPS: mps-to-circuit sequential (exact) circuits for the reference MPS truncated to χ∈{1,2,4,8,16}\chi\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}, LL-layer approximate circuits (L≤8L\le8), and generic state preparation of the exact ground state (N≤12N\le12). Each is measured against the exact ground state; the baseline for a target ε\varepsilon is the cheapest with infidelity ≤ε\le\varepsilon.

4. Q1: Can the filter be certified without knowing the exact ground state?

Short answer: yes, conditionally. Every quantity in the certificate, γ\gamma, Δ\Delta, WW, η\eta, F(0)F(0), α\alpha and TT, can be computed from DMRG output and the circuit, with no access to the exact ground state. The certificate is then a theorem given γ\gamma and Δ\Delta (Appendix A), and it is only as safe as those two inputs.

4.2 The certificate computed from DMRG inputs was never violated

Across 97 simulated points (every case at the certified step count plus the nn-sweeps of Section 5), with the certificate computed from DMRG inputs only, the total certificate, its leakage term, its Trotter term, the triangle inequality, the energy bound ⟨H⟩−E0≤WD‾2\langle H\rangle-E_0\le W\bar D^2 and the success-rate bound Psucc≥γF(0)2P_{\mathrm{succ}}\ge\gamma F(0)^2 each held 97 out of 97 times. The exact ground state was used only afterwards, to measure DD. This checks the derivation and the code; the proofs are in Appendix A.

Answer to Q3. Yes, to a good approximation: the hybrid rule recovers all but a factor of about 2.0 of the empirically sufficient cost with no ground state, and met every target. What it gives up is the proof: its Trotter part is a measurement under an assumed scaling, not a bound.

8. Discussion

The three answers together. The filter can be certified without the exact ground state, as long as γ\gamma and Δ\Delta are not optimistic (Q1); the certificate is accurate where it matters for the distance, because leakage dominates and is tight, but its Trotter half is one to two orders of magnitude too large (Q2); and a measured nn-versus-2n2n Trotter estimate recovers nearly all of the practical cost without the ground state, at the price of being empirical (Q3). The recommended practice is therefore: certify γ\gamma, Δ\Delta and the leakage rigorously, choose nn by the hybrid rule, and report the certified count as the conservative fallback.

What it costs. At ε=10−2\varepsilon=10^{-2} the hybrid circuit costs roughly 10310^3–4×1044\times10^4 CX at N=6N=6–1616 against 3030–1,1001{,}100 for direct MPS preparation. The filter is not competitive on these chains.

When it could matter. The filter’s cost is set by the gap and the commutator constant, not by the state’s entanglement; direct preparation’s cost steps up each time the bond dimension must double. The data show the ratio narrowing at one such step (N=16N=16, J2=0J_2=0) but not crossing. A genuine crossover would need systems where the required MPS bond dimension grows quickly with size (critical systems at larger NN, or two dimensions) and where the filter can be run without paying for dense simulation. Neither is tested here.

Limitations.

  • Inputs. The certificate needs γ\gamma and Δ\Delta. Here DMRG supplies them to 10−810^{-8} and exact diagonalisation validates them (N≤16N\le16). At larger NN there is no validation, and Section 4.3 shows the certificate breaks if the gap is optimistic. Certifying Δ\Delta at large NN is the main open point.
  • Models. Two couplings of one chain.
  • Trotter order. First order only; higher-order formulas would shrink the Trotter term but need a different constant.
  • Noise. Not simulated; the triangle inequality gives an additive noise term.
  • Cost model. CX only, all-to-all (a line costs about 22–3×3\times more), no T-count, no synthesis error; the CX per step is for a Heisenberg bond decomposition into three Pauli rotations and is not the cheapest possible.
  • Simulation. Dense statevectors, so the large-NN reach of DMRG is not exercised, and the largest certified circuits are not simulated.
  • Hybrid rule. An empirical rule that assumed the 1/n1/n regime and had 25 test cases.

Reproducibility. All code is in simple_paper/: dmrg_inputs.py (DMRG front end and trial circuits), workflow.py (Hamiltonian, pulses, certificate), run_experiments.py (main grid), run_extras.py (trial states, wrong inputs, design choices, direct-preparation baseline), resources.py (CX counts), pinsker_check.py, make_report.py, build_paper.py; filter design is j1j2_filter/floor.py. They run in a Python 3.12 environment with numpy, scipy, quimb, qiskit and mps-to-circuit; set OMP_NUM_THREADS=1 when running processes in parallel. The full set (all tables here) runs in one to two hours on four cores. Seeds are fixed, including the Lanczos start vector used for the exact ground state at N≥12N\ge12 (a χ=2\chi=2 cut passes through degenerate Schmidt values, so γ\gamma is otherwise only reproducible to the third digit).

Appendix B. Where Pinsker’s inequality applies
