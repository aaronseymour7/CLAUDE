\title{Cost and scaling of cosine-filter ground-state preparation from a matrix-product state:\\
gap, overlap and target in the $J_1$--$J_2$ chain, with a comparison to QETU}

\author{Authors to be added}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
A common route to a ground state on a quantum computer compiles a classical trial state and removes its residual
excited weight with a quantum filter. We benchmark the cost of this route on the spin-$\tfrac12$ $J_1$--$J_2$
chain, with a matrix-product-state (MPS) trial and the one-ancilla cosine filter of Stetcu, Baroni and Carlson
(SBC), and compare plans certified by an a-priori bound with exact simulation of the Trotterized circuits on up to
16 qubits. A controlled study that holds the gap (0.30--0.55) and the overlap fixed separately at $N=8$--14 shows
that the gap sets the circuit depth and the overlap the repetitions, and gives fitted cost laws for both. The
certificate's overhead is almost all in its Trotter bound: the planned filter is within \fCMDtime$\times$ of the
length simulation needs, but the bounded step is 60--75 times finer than needed, and the certified cost is a median
\goCertRatioMed$\times$ the measured one. Certified plans extend to $N=48$. Against a Chebyshev (QETU) filter under
identical accounting, SBC is cheaper measured at $\epsilon\approx10^{-2}$, ties at $10^{-3}$ and loses from
$10^{-4}$, and certified it is not cheaper. The infidelity bound held in all \nSimOK{} simulated circuits, all at
$N\le14$ with exact inputs; for larger systems it is conditional on DMRG estimates of the gap and overlap, and a
10--25\% gap overestimate breaks the optimized and Chebyshev plans. Measured against compiling a better MPS, the
filter wins on chains only as a last-mile polisher (\actOneWins{} of \actOneTot{} targets) but at 27 of 28 targets on
small cylinders, where a snake MPS struggles; with certified plans it loses wherever an MPS reaches the target.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A common route to a ground state on a quantum computer starts classically: an approximate method
produces a trial state that can be compiled into a short circuit, and a quantum subroutine removes its
residual excited-state weight. For one-dimensional systems the natural classical engine is
DMRG~\cite{White1992,Schollwock2011}, which yields the ground-state energy $E_0$, the gap and a
matrix-product state (MPS) whose low-bond-dimension truncations compile exactly into staircase
circuits~\cite{Ran2020}. The quantum step can be phase estimation~\cite{Ge2019}, a polynomial filter
implemented by quantum singular-value transformation~\cite{Lin2020,Gilyen2019,Dong2022}, or a product of
cosine filters implemented with a single ancilla~\cite{Stetcu2023,Choi2021}. The last option, the projection
algorithm of Stetcu, Baroni and Carlson (SBC)~\cite{Stetcu2023}, needs little beyond time evolution: each
pulse is one controlled time evolution between ancilla rotations and a measurement, the filter is designed
classically, and the success probability is known in advance.

This paper is a cost benchmark of that workflow on the open $J_1$--$J_2$ Heisenberg chain. We ask what the
filter costs, in two-qubit gates, arbitrary rotations, evolution time and repetitions, both as certified by an
a-priori bound and as measured by exact simulation of the Trotterized circuit; what sets that cost; and how it
compares with a Chebyshev polynomial filter (QETU)~\cite{Dong2022} and with simply compiling a better MPS, under
identical accounting. The spectral gap
$\Delta$ and the trial overlap $\gamma$ normally change together with system size $N$ and coupling. We decouple
them: the gap is held fixed across $N$ by tuning $J_2$, and the overlap by a one-parameter rotation of the trial
circuit, so each can be varied while the other stays put.

\emph{Relation to prior work.} The filter is SBC's~\cite{Stetcu2023}; it shares its circuit with the rodeo
algorithm~\cite{Choi2021,Qian2021}, which draws the times at random. Our closed-form ``dyadic'' schedule, a
halving ladder of times, is the one SBC and Rule \emph{et al.} use for quantum-number
projection~\cite{Stetcu2023,Rule2024}, and for energy it is a finite form of the rodeo super-iteration of Cohen
and Oh~\cite{CohenOh2023}, recently generalized~\cite{Patkowski2026}. The Trotter bounds are those of Childs
\emph{et al.}~\cite{Childs2021}, the certificate follows the standard argument for polynomial
filters~\cite{Lin2020,Dong2022}, and the scaling of filter depth with $1/\Delta$ and of repetitions with
$1/\gamma$ is the known complexity of filter methods~\cite{Ge2019,Lin2020}. QETU has been costed with
Trotterized evolution for lattice gauge theories~\cite{Kane2023} and run on small Hubbard
models~\cite{Muller2024,Karacan2024}. Resource studies that compile a classical trial and then refine it exist for
quantum chemistry~\cite{Fomichev2024,Berry2025,Lee2025}, and Watanabe \emph{et al.}~\cite{Watanabe2026} stop an MPS
encoder early and finish with probabilistic imaginary-time evolution on Heisenberg chains. What is new here is (i) a controlled separation of gap and overlap at fixed
size, which turns the known scaling into measured constants and exponents, (ii) a measured, not only bounded,
account of every resource and its factorization into filter length, Trotter step and gates per step, which shows
where the certificate's overhead sits, and (iii) a like-for-like measured comparison of cosine and Chebyshev
filters, including the Chebyshev filter's query length, and (iv) a measured benchmark of filtering a cheap MPS
against compiling a better one, on chains and small cylinders. The method is not new; the contribution is the benchmark.
The code is the \texttt{sbpipe} package, available with all data at
\url{https://github.com/aaronseymour7/FilterPipeline}; every number below is produced by the scripts named in
Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

Section~\ref{sec:pipeline} describes the pipeline and the certificate, Sec.~\ref{sec:application} the model and
the validation, Sec.~\ref{sec:gapov} the controlled gap/overlap study and its cost laws, Sec.~\ref{sec:regime}
certified resources to $N=48$ and their sensitivity to wrong inputs, Sec.~\ref{sec:crossover} the benchmark against
the MPS alone, Sec.~\ref{sec:qetu} the comparison with QETU,
and Sec.~\ref{sec:meascert} the gap between measured and certified cost.

\subsection{Inputs and the spectral window}

The pipeline takes a Pauli-sum Hamiltonian $H=\sum_ac_aP_a$, an ansatz, and energy estimates with error bars,
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
E_0\in[E_0^{\rm est}-\delta E_0,\;E_0^{\rm est}+\delta E_0],\\
E_1\ge E_1^{\rm est}-\delta E_1,\qquad E_{\rm top}\le E_{\max},
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:assumptions}
\end{equation}
where $E_1$ is the lowest level above the ground space that the trial state can overlap with; for trial states
that are not symmetry-adapted, as here, this is the global first excited level. With an overestimated $E_1$ a
real excitation can sit in the filter's transition region and the bound says nothing (Sec.~\ref{sec:sens}). If
no $E_{\max}$ is given, the rigorous Weyl bound $E_{\max}=\sum_g\lambda_{\max}(h_g)$ over term groups $h_g$ is
used. With $s=E_0^{\rm est}-\delta E_0$ and $W=E_{\max}-s$, the scaled Hamiltonian $H_s=(H-s)/W$ has its ground
energy in the \emph{pass band} $[0,w_0]$, $w_0=2\delta E_0/W$, and every other relevant level in the
\emph{stop band} $[\Delta,1]$, $\Delta=(E_1^{\rm est}-\delta E_1-s)/W$. The ansatz enters through $\gamma$, a
lower estimate of its ground-space weight, and the gate cost of its circuit.

\subsection{The filter and three schedules}

One SBC pulse~\cite{Stetcu2023} applies $\mathsf H_a\,e^{-itH_s\otimes Z_a}\,R_z^{(a)}(2\phi)\,\mathsf H_a$
to the system and an ancilla in $\ket0$; its ancilla-$\ket0$ block is $\cos(H_st+\phi)$. Keeping outcome 0
after $m$ pulses applies
\begin{equation}
F(H_s)=\prod_{i=1}^m\cos(H_st_i+\phi_i),\qquad T=\sum_i|t_i|.
\end{equation}
A filter is characterized by its pass-band floor $F_{\rm pass}\le\min_{[0,w_0]}|F|$ and suppression ratio
$\eta=\sup_{[\Delta,1]}|F|/F_{\rm pass}$, both certified rather than sampled (by interval branch-and-bound
using $|F'|\le T$, $|F''|\le T^2$, or in closed form). For a target leakage $\epsilon_\ell$ the design target is
$\eta^2\le\gamma\epsilon_\ell/[(1-\epsilon_\ell)(1-\gamma)]$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:leak}). Three schedules are
provided.

\emph{Uniform}: $m$ pulses $t=\pi/(1+\Delta)$, $\phi=0$. On the stop band $Et\in[\theta,\pi-\theta]$,
$\theta=\pi\Delta/(1+\Delta)$, so $|\cos Et|\le\cos\theta$ and $T\approx2\ln(1/\eta)/(\pi\Delta^2)$, quadratic in
the inverse gap; it is the baseline.

\emph{Dyadic}: $r$ repetitions of the halving ladder $t=\tau,2\tau,\dots,2^{K-1}\tau$, $\tau=\pi/(1+\Delta)$,
$\phi=0$. One block is a Dirichlet kernel,
\begin{equation}
\prod_{k=0}^{K-1}\cos(2^kE\tau)=\frac{\sin(2^KE\tau)}{2^K\sin(E\tau)},
\label{eq:dirichlet}
\end{equation}
exact for every $E$ and equal to 1 at $E=0$. On the stop band $\sin(E\tau)\ge\sin\theta$, so a block is at most
$\eta_b=1/(2^K\sin\theta)$ there; this uses the scaling $H_s$ and the choice of $\tau$, and does not hold outside
$[\Delta,1]$. With $r=\lceil\ln\eta/\ln\eta_b\rceil$ blocks and $2^K\sin\theta\approx e$,
\begin{equation}
T_{\rm dyadic}\approx\frac{e\,\tau}{\sin\theta}\ln\frac1\eta\;\approx\;\frac{e}{\Delta}\ln\frac1\eta ,
\end{equation}
the optimal $O(\Delta^{-1}\log\eta^{-1})$ scaling~\cite{Lin2020,CohenOh2023} with an explicit constant and no
optimization. The pipeline searches $K$ and $r$ for the smallest $T$.

\emph{Optimized}: times and phases that maximize $F(0)^2$ subject to the certified constraint on $\eta$ at fixed
$(T,m)$, over a grid of $T$ and $m\in\{4,6,8\}$. This is SBC's energy recipe~\cite{Stetcu2023} with a certified
constraint; nonzero phases trade success probability for a shorter $T$.

For each schedule the leakage share $\epsilon_\ell/\epsilon\in\{0.5,0.25,0.1,0.05\}$ is scanned and the plan with
the fewest expected CNOTs per success is kept; an ``auto'' mode does the same over the dyadic and optimized
schedules together.

\subsection{Trotterization}

Each controlled evolution $e^{-it_iH_s\otimes Z_a}$ is approximated by $k_i$ first-order (Lie) or second-order
(Strang) steps over the non-identity terms; the identity term becomes one exact ancilla $R_z$ per pulse. With
$B_a=\sum_{b>a}H_b$, the bounds of Childs \emph{et al.}~\cite[Props.~15--16]{Childs2021} are
\begin{align}
\norm{S_1(t)-e^{-itH}}&\le\tfrac{t^2}{2}\alpha_1,&\alpha_1&=\textstyle\sum_a\norm{[B_a,H_a]},\\
\norm{S_2(t)-e^{-itH}}&\le t^3\beta_2,&\beta_2&=\textstyle\frac1{12}\sum_a\norm{[B_a,[B_a,H_a]]}\nonumber\\
&&&\quad+\textstyle\frac1{24}\sum_a\norm{[H_a,[H_a,B_a]]},
\end{align}
evaluated for the terms of $H_s$ (equivalently $\alpha_1(H)/W^2$ and $\beta_2(H)/W^3$ with physical time $t_i/W$);
tensoring with $Z_a$ leaves them unchanged. Each commutator is a short Pauli sum, and its norm is computed exactly
on its support (dense up to 8 qubits, Lanczos to $10^{-12}$ up to 12). Every pulse evolves under the same
$H_s\otimes Z_a$, so the per-pulse bounds share one constant, and minimizing $\sum_ik_i$ at fixed total error gives
$k_i\propto|t_i|$; we take $k_i=\lceil|t_i|/\delta t\rceil$ with the common step $\delta t$ of that solution.

\subsection{The certificate}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:cert}
Assume \eqref{eq:assumptions} and that the trial state has ground-space weight at least $\gamma$. Let
the designed filter have certified $\eta$ and $F_{\rm pass}$, let $\epsilon_U$ bound the operator-norm
error of the implementation (Trotter error $\sum_i\epsilon_i$ plus $R\delta$ for the $R$ arbitrary
rotations of the trial and filter circuits, each synthesized to accuracy $\delta$), and let
\begin{equation}
\ell=\frac{(1-\gamma)\eta^2}{\gamma+(1-\gamma)\eta^2},\qquad p_g=\gamma F_{\rm pass}^2 .
\end{equation}
Let $\ket g=\Pi_0\ket\psi/\norm{\Pi_0\ket\psi}$ be the normalized projection of the trial state onto
the ground space; $\ket g$ is pure also when the ground space is degenerate. For a noiseless implementation the
state $\ket{\tilde\psi_{\rm out}}$ prepared conditioned on the ancilla outcomes 0 is pure, and
\begin{equation}
D\le\sqrt\ell+\frac{\epsilon_U}{\sqrt{p_g}},\qquad
1-\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi_0\tilde\rho)\le1-|\langle g|\tilde\psi_{\rm out}\rangle|^2=D^2,
\label{eq:cert}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is the trace distance between $\tilde\rho=\ket{\tilde\psi_{\rm out}}\bra{\tilde\psi_{\rm out}}$
and $\ket g$. The success probability is at least $(\sqrt{p_g}-\epsilon_U)^2$. Below, $1-F$ denotes
$1-|\langle g|\tilde\psi_{\rm out}\rangle|^2$, the usual infidelity for the nondegenerate chains studied here.
\end{theorem}

The proof (Appendix~\ref{app:proof}) combines a leakage lemma for the exact filter with the observation that a
vector error $\epsilon_U$ on an unnormalized vector of norm at least $\sqrt{p_g}$ moves its direction by at most
$\epsilon_U/\sqrt{p_g}$. The pipeline sets $\epsilon_U=(\sqrt\epsilon-\sqrt\ell)\sqrt{p_g}$, 90\% of it for the
product formula and the rest for rotation synthesis. If the trial already satisfies $1-\gamma\le\epsilon$, the
empty filter is offered too.

\subsection{Resource model}

We assume all-to-all connectivity and Clifford+$R_z$ gates. For a Heisenberg bond $(i,j)$ the three commuting
controlled terms form one exact block,
\begin{align}
e^{-ict(X_iX_j+Y_iY_j+Z_iZ_j)\otimes Z_a}
&=V^\dagger e^{-ict(Z_iZ_a+Z_jZ_a-Z_iZ_jZ_a)}V,\nonumber\\
V&=\mathsf H_i\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{ij},
\label{eq:bond}
\end{align}
whose diagonal factor is a phase polynomial on three parities~\cite{Amy2014}: 6 CNOTs and 3 $R_z$, against 12
CNOTs for three separate Pauli rotations and 8 for qiskit's \texttt{PauliEvolutionGate} (checked in the test
suite). The block is the same unitary as its three term rotations, so the Trotter error is that of the term-wise
formula. Control by an anticommuting Pauli string, as in controlled-reversal rodeo
circuits~\cite{BeeLindgren2025} and control-free QETU~\cite{Dong2022}, is not available, because no Pauli product
anticommutes with all of $XX$, $YY$ and $ZZ$ on a bond. $k$ Strang steps cost $k(2B-2)+1$ blocks for $B$ bonds;
each pulse adds one ancilla $R_z$. An arbitrary rotation at accuracy $\delta$ costs $3\log_2(1/\delta)$ $T$
gates~\cite{Ross2016}. The expected cost per success is the cost of one attempt (trial plus filter) divided by the
certified success probability $(\sqrt{p_g}-\epsilon_U)^2\ge(1-\sqrt\epsilon)^2p_g$ (repeat-until-success; early
abort is not modeled).

\section{Model, inputs and validation}
\label{sec:application}

We study the open spin-$\frac12$ chain
\begin{equation}
H=J_1\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_{i+1}+J_2\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_{i+2},
\end{equation}
$J_1=1$, at $J_2=0$ (Heisenberg), $J_2=0.2411$ (near the gapless-to-dimerized
transition~\cite{Okamoto1992,Eggert1996}) and $J_2=0.4$ (dimerized), for $N=8$ to 48; it has $B=N-1$ bonds at
$J_2=0$ and $B=2N-3$ otherwise. The front end runs two-site DMRG (quimb~\cite{Gray2018}) with bond dimensions up
to 256; $E_1$ is the lowest triplet, from a penalty run. The error bars are the energy change between the last two
bond dimensions, a convergence heuristic, not a bound. Both DMRG energies are variational upper bounds, so a
residual error in $E_1$ biases the gap upward, the dangerous direction. The trial is the variationally optimal MPS
at bond dimension $\chi\in\{1,2,4,8\}$, and $\gamma$ is its overlap with the reference MPS, an estimate rather than
a lower bound. An MPS of bond dimension $2^b$ is a staircase of $N-b$ unitaries on $b+1$ qubits, each costed by the
quantum Shannon decomposition bound~\cite{Shende2006}. For $N\le14$, exact diagonalization (ED) reproduces $E_0$ to
$\dmrgEzeroErr$, the first excited level to $\dmrgEoneErr$ (always the triplet) and the overlaps to
$\dmrgGammaErr$ (Table~\ref{tab:inputs}). Beyond $N=14$ the certificate is conditional on the DMRG gap and overlap.

\subsection{Validation of the certificate ($N\le14$)}
\label{sec:validation}

For $N\in\{8,10,12,14\}$, the three couplings, $\epsilon\in\{10^{-2},10^{-3}\}$, three schedules and both orders,
we plan a circuit for the $\chi=2$ trial and, where feasible, simulate it exactly against the ED ground state. Of
\nPlans{} plans, \nSim{} were simulated (the rest, mostly uniform or first-order plans with $10^{4}$--$10^{8}$
steps, exceed our budget), and the certificate held in all \nSimOK{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}).

The bound is conservative by a median factor \boundRatioMed{} (range \boundRatioMin--$\boundRatioMax$) in
infidelity. The leakage term overestimates the exact filter's infidelity by a median \leakRatioMed$\times$: 1.4
for the optimized schedule, which pushes $|F|$ to $\eta F_{\rm pass}$ across the band, and 54 for the dyadic one,
whose closed-form bound is reached only near the band edge. The Trotter term overestimates the measured Trotter
distance by a median \trotRatioMed$\times$ (range \trotRatioMin--$\trotRatioMax$): commutator bounds are
worst-case over all states, while the filtered state lives in the low-energy subspace, where Trotter errors are
much smaller~\cite{Sahinoglu2021,Mizuta2025,Childs2018}. The measured success probability equals $p_g$ to two

\section{Gap and overlap as independent inputs}
\label{sec:gapov}

Along any natural family of chains the gap and the overlap move together: at fixed $J_2$ the gap closes with $N$,
and at fixed $\chi$ the overlap decays with $N$. We therefore construct a family in which $\Delta$ and $\gamma$ are
set independently at every $N$.

\emph{Construction.} For each $N\in\{8,10,12,14\}$ and target gap $g=E_1-E_0\in\{0.30,0.35,\dots,0.55\}$, $J_2$
is found by bisection on ED so that the singlet--triplet gap equals $g$ to $10^{-9}$ (pairs without a solution
below the dimerized regime are left out). For all tuned couplings the lowest $S^z=0$ excitation coincides with the
lowest triplet to $10^{-7}$, so $g$ is the true gap. The trial is the variational $\chi=16$ MPS, prepared by its
staircase circuit, followed by $R_y(\theta)$ on every even site; rotating one sublattice breaks the singlet
symmetry and lowers $\gamma$ smoothly, and $\theta$ is set by bisection to hit the target $\gamma$. The trial
circuit is therefore the same along an overlap scan. Study A fixes $g\in\{0.40,0.50\}$ and scans
$\gamma\in\{0.03,0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$; study B fixes $\gamma\in\{0.1,0.5\}$ and scans $g$; both at
$\epsilon\in\{10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4}\}$, \goNcases{} cases in all, each with the dyadic and optimized schedules
and with QETU (Sec.~\ref{sec:qetu}).

\emph{Measured and certified costs.} \emph{Measured}: for the shortest filters that reach $\epsilon$ exactly (dyadic
ladders, or SBC's optimized design for the true gap and overlap), the coarsest common Strang step at which the
Trotterized circuit still reaches $\epsilon$ is found by bisection on the state vector. Optimized designs that
barely pass the ground state ($p<\gamma/2$) are repeated over seeds (\optRetried{} of \optPoints{} points).
\emph{Certified}: Theorem~\ref{thm:cert} with exact energies and the true $\gamma$. Costs are per shot and per
success, for the filter alone unless stated.

\subsection{The gap sets the circuit, the overlap sets the repetitions}

Figures~\ref{fig:filterA} and~\ref{fig:filterB} show CNOTs, $R_z$ rotations and evolution time per shot, and CNOTs
per success, along both scans.

\emph{Per shot, the overlap enters only logarithmically.} At fixed gap, lowering $\gamma$ thirtyfold (0.9 to 0.03)
raises the measured CNOTs per shot of the dyadic filter by only \fAMDcnot$\times$ and its evolution time by
\fAMDtime$\times$. The filter must reach $\eta^2=\gamma\epsilon/(1-\gamma)$, and a worse trial adds only
$\ln290\approx5.7$ to $L=\ln[(1-\gamma)/(\gamma\epsilon)]$, already 2--13 here. The dyadic filter grows in whole
pulses, so its per-shot cost moves in steps.

\emph{Per shot, the gap enters as a power.} At fixed overlap, closing the gap by $1.8\times$ (0.55 to 0.30) raises
the CNOTs per shot by \fBMDcnot$\times$ and the evolution time by \fBMDtime$\times$, about as $1/\Delta$. Both
behaviors are the textbook $T=O(\Delta^{-1}\ln\eta^{-1})$~\cite{Lin2020,Ge2019}; the controlled study shows that
the measured circuits follow it at fixed $N$ with the inputs varied separately, and what the gates per shot and
the Trotter step add.

\emph{Per success, the overlap returns as $1/\gamma$.} Every run succeeds with probability $p\approx\gamma
f(E_0)^2$, with $f(E_0)=1$ for the dyadic filter, so the cost per success carries $1/\gamma$. The gap decides the
depth of the coherent circuit; the overlap decides how often it has to be run.

\emph{$R_z$ counts follow CNOT counts.} Each bond block has 3 $R_z$ and 6 CNOTs and each pulse adds one rotation,
so $R_z/\mathrm{CNOT}=\fRzCnot$ throughout; statements about CNOTs hold for the $T$ count up to the synthesis factor.

\subsection{Mechanism: filter length, Trotter step and gates per step}

The per-shot cost factorizes as
\begin{equation}
\text{CNOTs per shot}=\frac{T}{\delta t}\times c_{\rm step}(N),
\label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
and each factor answers to different inputs (Fig.~\ref{fig:mech}).

\emph{Filter length.} The physical evolution time depends on $\Delta$ and $L$ only: fits give
$T\propto\Delta^{-\fitMDTgap}L^{\fitMDTL}N^{\fitMDTN}$ measured and
$T\propto\Delta^{-\fitCDTgap}L^{\fitCDTL}N^{\fitCDTN}$ certified, so at fixed absolute gap the filter length is
independent of $N$. The certified length is a median \fCMDtime$\times$ the measured one (range
\fCMDtimeLo--\fCMDtimeHi): the certificate sizes the filter nearly right.

\emph{Trotter step.} The measured dyadic filter tolerates coarse steps, median $\delta t=\fDtDTwo$, \fDtDThree,
\fDtDFour{} (in $1/J_1$) at $\epsilon=10^{-2}$, $10^{-3}$, $10^{-4}$: its pulses are long and smooth, and the
filtered state lives where Trotter errors are small~\cite{Sahinoglu2021}. The certified step must cover the worst
case over the whole evolution, median $\delta t=\fDtCTwo$, \fDtCThree, \fDtCFour, about 60--75 times finer and
shrinking as $T$ grows. The certified step count therefore grows as $L^{\fitCDSL}$ against $L^{\fitMDSL}$ measured
and is a median \fCMDsteps$\times$ the measured one. Almost all of the certificate's overhead is in the Trotter
bound, not in the filter.

\emph{Gates per step.} One Strang step costs a median $\fCps\,N$ CNOTs. The measured step count barely grows with
$N$ (exponent \fitMDSN), so measured CNOTs grow as $N^{\fitMDCN}$; the certified step count grows as $N^{\fitCDSN}$
through the commutator constant and the scaled time, giving $N^{\fitCDCN}$.

\subsection{Cost laws}

Fitting the expected CNOTs per success (filter and trial) to
\begin{equation}
C\approx a\,N^{\alpha}\,\Delta^{-\beta}\,L^{\lambda}\,\gamma^{-g},\qquad L=\ln\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma\epsilon},
\label{eq:fit}
\end{equation}
gives Table~\ref{tab:gofit}. For the dyadic filter,
\begin{align}
C_{\rm meas}&\approx\goMDa\,N^{\goMDN}\Delta^{-\goMDgap}L^{\goMDL}\gamma^{-\goMDgam},\\
C_{\rm cert}&\approx\goCDa\,N^{\goCDN}\Delta^{-\goCDgap}L^{\goCDL}\gamma^{-\goCDgam},
\end{align}
within a typical factor of \goMDrms{} and \goCDrms. The certified law has the $1/\gamma$ of the repetitions and an
$L^{2.4}$ from the Trotter bound; the measured law grows more slowly in both $N$ and $L$. Its $\gamma$ exponent is
below one because at coarse steps each Trotterized pulse moves some weight into or out of the ground state
($p/\gamma$ ranges from 0.76 to 1.24). The certified cost lies \goCertRatioMin--\goCertRatioMax$\times$ (median
\goCertRatioMed) above the measured one. These exponents summarize $N=8$--14 and $g=0.30$--0.55 and are not
asymptotic laws. Appendix~\ref{app:dyaopt} compares the two SBC schedules and maps the cost over the
$(\Delta,\gamma)$ plane.

\subsection{What if the energy estimates are wrong?}
\label{sec:sens}

The certificate is only as good as its inputs. We perturb them for $N=12$, $J_2=0.2411$, $\epsilon=10^{-2}$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}) and, for the gap and overlap, for every $N\le14$, coupling and schedule
(Table~\ref{tab:senswide}), and simulate the resulting circuits on the true trial state.

\emph{Gap.} An assumed gap larger than the true one moves the stop-band edge above the first excitation. With exact
inputs all \sensGapN{} plans meet the target; a 10\% overestimate breaks it in \sensGapOneTenMiss{} (marginally,
worst \sensGapOneTenWorst), 25\% in \sensGapOneQuarterMiss{} and 50\% in \sensGapOneHalfMiss. The dyadic schedule is
the most tolerant (\sensDyaQuarterMiss{} misses at 25\%) because its bound is loosest; the optimized and Chebyshev
filters have no slack. Underestimating the gap is safe and cheap: assuming \gapMarginF{} of it costs
\gapMarginCost$\times$.

\emph{Ground energy and overlap.} An error bar $\delta E_0$ lowers $F_{\rm pass}$, and the cost stays flat until it
is a sizeable fraction of the gap. Underestimating $\gamma$ is safe. Overestimating it broke the target in
\sensGammaMiss{} of \sensGammaN{} plans, all because the inflated $\gamma$ crossed $1-\epsilon$ and the pipeline
dropped the filter; the empty filter should be accepted only on a certified lower bound on $\gamma$.

\emph{A margin from DMRG convergence.} Setting $\delta E_0$ and $\delta E_1$ to the change between the last two bond
dimensions of the DMRG ladder covered both exact energies in \mgCov{} of \mgN{} tests on \mgSys{} systems with exact
spectra, against \mgCovRaw{} without error bars, and all \mbPlans{} plans built from these ladders met the target.
Once DMRG has converged the margin costs a median \mbHiMed$\times$; when it has not, the energy window becomes
inconsistent and the pipeline reports that instead of a plan.

\section{Benchmark against the MPS alone}
\label{sec:crossover}

The alternative to filtering a cheap trial is compiling a better MPS. We compare the two at equal target
infidelity, measured on both sides: for each system the exact spectrum is computed per $S^z$ block, every MPS trial
with $\chi=2,\dots,64$ is decomposed on it, and for each $\epsilon\in[10^{-5},10^{-2}]$ the cheapest route is found:
the smallest MPS that meets $1-\gamma\le\epsilon$ alone, against the cheapest smaller MPS followed by a measured SBC
or QETU filter (shortest exact filter, then the coarsest passing Strang step), with cost (trial $+$ filter)$/p$. Both
routes use the staircase MPS cost of Sec.~\ref{sec:application}.

\emph{Chains.} On nine chains ($N=12$--16, three couplings; Fig.~\ref{fig:actual1d}) compiling the MPS is cheaper in
most cases: the filtered route wins \actOneWins{} of \actOneTot{} targets, by 1.1--\actOneRatioMax$\times$. Every win
filters a trial that misses the target by $(1-\gamma)/\epsilon\le\actOneFarMax$, with a dyadic ladder of one to four
pulses (1--21 Strang steps, $p\ge0.988$), and all \actOneFar{} candidate routes with a larger ratio lose, by a median
\actOneFarMed$\times$. The filter acts as a last-mile polisher: MPS cost jumps about fourfold per doubling of $\chi$,
so a target just below what a given $\chi$ reaches forces the MPS-alone route up a bond dimension, while a short
filter removes the small residual of the lower one. The wins are therefore tied to the bond-dimension grid rather
than to $N$ or $J_2$. The optimized design never wins, because certifying suppression on the whole stop band needs
$T\gtrsim\pi/\Delta$ where one dyadic pulse suffices, and QETU beats the MPS alone at 11 targets but is never the
cheapest route.

\emph{Cylinders.} On $4\times3$ and $4\times4$ cylinders (periodic around the four-site circumference) at $J_2=0$ and
0.3, with snake-path MPS trials (Fig.~\ref{fig:actual}), the picture reverses. A snake MPS struggles here: at
$4\times4$, $J_2=0$, $\chi=16$ misses $\epsilon=10^{-2}$ ($1-\gamma=1.1\times10^{-2}$), $\chi=32$ stalls at
$7\times10^{-4}$ and no $\chi\le64$ reaches $10^{-5}$, while the compile cost grows about fourfold per doubling
(5\,328, 20\,548 and 76\,600 CNOTs for $\chi=16$, 32, 64). The filtered route is cheaper at 27 of the 28 cylinder
targets, by 1.1--\actRatioMax$\times$, and is the only MPS-based route at $10^{-5}$ on $4\times4$. Every win uses the
dyadic ladder, mostly on a $\chi=16$ trial, with $p\ge0.77$ and 1--46 Strang steps, and recovers factors
$(1-\gamma)/\epsilon$ up to $10^3$. The filter's depth grows only as $\ln(1/\epsilon)$ at fixed gap, while the MPS
needed to reach $\epsilon$ alone grows fourfold per bond-dimension step. All trials are snake MPS, a weak choice in
two dimensions; a better ansatz would help both routes. The staircase cost charges every site a unitary on $b+1$
qubits even where the bond is smaller, which favors the filtered route, most where its trial is cheap.

\emph{Certified.} With certified filters the comparison is lost: the filtered route costs a median
\certOneOverMed$\times$ the MPS alone on the chains (never below \certOneOverMin$\times$) and a median
\certTwoOverMed$\times$ on the cylinders wherever some $\chi\le64$ reaches $\epsilon$, winning only where no MPS does
(Table~\ref{tab:meascert}). The measured advantage on cylinders is a statement about what the circuit needs;
claiming it with a guarantee requires the tighter Trotter bound discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:meascert}.

\section{Comparison with a Chebyshev filter (QETU)}
\label{sec:qetu}

The pipeline also designs the quantum eigenvalue transformation of unitaries (QETU)~\cite{Dong2022}: $d$ queries of
$e^{-i\tau H_s\otimes Z_a}$ interleaved with ancilla rotations and a single post-selection, whose ancilla-0 block is
$p(\cos\tau H_s)$ for a real even polynomial $p$ of degree $d$ with $|p|\le1$. With $\tau=\pi/2$ the stop band maps
to $[0,x_s]$, $x_s=\cos(\pi\Delta/2)$, and we take the extremal Chebyshev filter
\begin{equation}
p(x)=\frac{T_d(x/x_s)}{T_d(1/x_s)},\qquad \eta=\frac1{T_d(\cos(\tau w_0)/x_s)},
\end{equation}
certified in closed form, with $p(0)=1$ and $T=d\tau\approx\Delta^{-1}\ln(2/\eta)$, two to three times less than the
dyadic ladder. The Trotter bound, step allocation and Theorem~\ref{thm:cert} carry over unchanged; the classical
phase-finding step is not costed, and QETU is costed in the controlled form (Sec.~\ref{sec:pipeline}).

\emph{Certified.} QETU is feasible in all \qetuN{} second-order scaling cases and a median \qetuDyaMed$\times$
(range \qetuDyaMin--\qetuDyaMax) the cost of the dyadic schedule; against the optimized design the ratio is a median
\qetuOptMed, the two being equal within the spread because both reach $T\approx\Delta^{-1}\ln(1/\eta)$ and the
Trotter cost depends only on $T$. The certificate held in \qetuNSimOK{} of \qetuNSim{} simulated QETU plans, with a
nearly tight leakage term (median \qetuLeakMed$\times$). In the controlled study the certified QETU plan is cheaper
than the certified dyadic one in all \cQetuWinsDy{} cases and the cheapest certified filter in \cbestQeTwo,
\cbestQeThree{} and \cbestQeFour{} of 74 at $\epsilon=10^{-2}$, $10^{-3}$, $10^{-4}$. Certified, SBC offers no cost
advantage over a polynomial filter; it avoids phase finding and measures after every pulse, which an early-abort
protocol could exploit.

\emph{Measured.} At $\tau=\pi/2$ QETU is cheaper in \goQetuWinsTwo{} of \goCasesTwo{} cases at $\epsilon=10^{-2}$,
\goQetuWinsThree{} of \goCasesThree{} at $10^{-3}$ and \goQetuWinsFour{} of \goCasesFour{} at $10^{-4}$. The reason
is the Trotter step: QETU needs less evolution time (median \fQDtime$\times$ dyadic), but takes one fine Strang step
per short query, $\delta t=\tau/W\propto1/N$, so its step count is a median \fQDsteps$\times$ the dyadic one and its
CNOTs grow as $N^{\fitMQCN}$ against $N^{\fitMDCN}$. The long dyadic pulses tolerate steps of order $1/J_1$, but
these shrink as roughly $\epsilon^{0.3}$.

\emph{Query length.} Any $\tau\le\pi/(1+\Delta)$ keeps the excited levels inside $|\cos\tau E|\le\cos\tau\Delta$, so
we redid every measured QETU search at $\tau=3\pi/4$ and $\pi/(1+\Delta)$ (\tauNcases{} cases). One Strang step per
query still suffices, so the cost falls to a median \tauPiMed{} of the $\tau=\pi/2$ cost at $\tau=\pi/(1+\Delta)$.
With QETU at its best $\tau$ (Table~\ref{tab:qetutau}) the SBC filters keep $\epsilon=10^{-2}$ (\tauBestQeTwo{}
QETU wins of 74), the two split $10^{-3}$ (\tauBestQeThree{} of 74), and QETU wins \tauBestQeFour{} of 74 at
$10^{-4}$. Appendix~\ref{app:empirical} analyzes what sets the Chebyshev filter's degree.

\section{Measured versus certified}
\label{sec:meascert}

Every cost above has two versions (Table~\ref{tab:meascert}). The measured cost picks the shortest filter and the
coarsest Trotter step that reach $\epsilon$, which needs the exact ground state; it shows what the circuit has to do.
The certified cost is what the pipeline plans from Theorem~\ref{thm:cert} without that knowledge. They differ mainly
through the Trotter step, and the difference decides which filter is cheapest: measured, the long SBC pulses win at
loose targets; certified, every filter pays the same worst-case step per unit time, the shorter Chebyshev filter
wins more often, and only the optimized SBC design competes.

To isolate the step, we keep the filter as planned and Trotterize it on a common grid $\delta t=4\cdot2^{-n}/J_1$ for
every chain ($N=12$--16) and small-cylinder target and both schedules (\apN{} cases; \texttt{run\_aposteriori.py}).
With the coarsest passing step the planned filter costs a median \apOrCertS{} (SBC) and \apOrCertQ{} (QETU) of its
certified cost (Table~\ref{tab:apost}): the step, not the filter, is almost the whole overhead. A check that needs
neither the bound nor the ground state, halving $\delta t$ until the post-selected energy changes by less than
$\epsilon\Delta/2$, met the target in \apEok{} of \apEtot{} cases at a median \apEcertS{} of the certified cost, but
its calibration takes a median $\apShotsMed$ post-selected shots, growing as $\epsilon^{-2}$. Tighter Trotter bounds,
for example restricted to the low-energy subspace~\cite{Sahinoglu2021,Mizuta2025}, are what would close the gap; a
better filter design would not.

\section{Discussion}

\emph{What sets the cost.} The gap determines the coherent circuit: evolution time $\propto L/\Delta$, independent of
$N$ at fixed gap, and CNOTs and $R_z$ per shot that scale as a power of $1/\Delta$. The overlap enters the circuit
only through a logarithm and the expected cost through $1/\gamma$, so the filter tolerates a poor trial by paying in
repetitions rather than depth; amplitude amplification~\cite{Brassard2002,Yoder2014} would turn that into
$1/\sqrt\gamma$ at the price of a deeper coherent circuit, and is not modeled. The target enters through $L$ and,
for SBC, through the Trotter step, $\delta t\propto\epsilon^{0.3}$.

\emph{Against the MPS alone.} A filter pays where the trial is hard to improve. For chains the MPS infidelity
falls quickly with $\chi$, so the filter helps only as a last-mile polisher; on small cylinders, where a snake MPS
needs a fast-growing bond dimension, it wins almost everywhere, measured. Certified, it does not, which again points
to the Trotter bound as the place to improve.

\emph{Dyadic, optimized or Chebyshev.} Measured, SBC wins at $\epsilon\approx10^{-2}$ because its long smooth
pulses tolerate coarse steps; QETU at its longest admissible query ties at $10^{-3}$ and wins from $10^{-4}$, and
certified it is usually the cheaper filter. The optimized SBC design is cheaper than the dyadic ladder at
$\epsilon\le10^{-3}$ (median \odMMed$\times$ measured, \odCMed$\times$ certified) but occasionally fails; the dyadic
ladder is the robust fallback, which is how the ``auto'' mode uses them.

\emph{What is guaranteed.} Theorem~\ref{thm:cert} holds whenever its inputs are right. The Weyl bound and the filter
certificate are rigorous, and the Trotter constants are exact up to the Lanczos tolerance. The interval for $E_0$,
the bound on $E_1$ and the overlap come from DMRG and are well-converged estimates, not bounds; for $N\le14$ ED
confirms them, and beyond that the certificate is conditional on all three. The gap is the critical input, and
because its DMRG bias runs in the dangerous direction a deployable plan should carry an explicit margin $\delta E_1$,
which costs little beside the Trotter overhead. Rigorous lower bounds on $E_1$ (e.g.\ Temple-type) and the Eckart
inequality $1-\gamma\le(\langle H\rangle_\psi-E_0)/(E_1-E_0)$ would remove these conditions.

\emph{Limitations.} Noiseless simulation; all-to-all connectivity; repeat-until-success rather than early abort;
trial circuits costed by a generic decomposition bound; exact simulation and the controlled study only to
$N=14$--16, sizes at which classical methods are exact, so the fitted exponents are descriptive and claims about
larger systems rest on certified plans; and a fixable gap range (0.30--0.55) in the easy regime, well above the
gaps near 0.07 of the natural chains at $N=48$.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cert}}
\label{app:proof}

Write the trial state as $\ket\psi=\sqrt{\gamma'}\ket g+\sqrt{1-\gamma'}\ket\chi$ with $\ket g$ in the
ground space (energy $e_0\in[0,w_0]$ in scaled units), $\ket\chi$ orthogonal to it and $\gamma'\ge\gamma$.

\begin{lemma}[leakage]\label{lem:leak}
For the exact filter, $v=F(H_s)\ket\psi$ has weight outside the ground space at most $\ell$, and
$\norm v^2\ge p_g$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$F(H_s)$ is a function of $H_s$, so $v=\sqrt{\gamma'}F(e_0)\ket g+\sqrt{1-\gamma'}F(H_s)\ket\chi$ with
the two parts orthogonal. Every component of $\ket\chi$ has energy in $[\Delta,1]$, so
$\norm{F(H_s)\chi}\le\sup_{[\Delta,1]}|F|\le\eta F_{\rm pass}\le\eta|F(e_0)|$. The excited weight is
$x/(a+x)$ with $a=\gamma'F(e_0)^2$, $x=(1-\gamma')\norm{F\chi}^2\le(1-\gamma')\eta^2F(e_0)^2$; it is
increasing in $x$ and, after dividing by $F(e_0)^2$, decreasing in $\gamma'$, which gives $\ell$.
Finally $\norm v^2\ge a\ge\gamma F_{\rm pass}^2$.
\end{proof}

For pure states the trace distance is $\sin\angle$, so $D(\hat v,\ket g)=\sqrt{\ell'}\le\sqrt\ell$
($\hat v=v/\norm v$).

\begin{lemma}[implementation error]
Let $B_i=\frac12(e^{-i\phi_i}\tilde S_i(t_i)+e^{i\phi_i}\tilde S_i(-t_i))$ be the ancilla-0 block of the
implemented pulse and $C_i=\cos(H_st_i+\phi_i)$. Then
$\norm{\prod_iB_i-\prod_iC_i}\le\epsilon_U$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$C_i=\frac12(e^{-i\phi_i}e^{-it_iH_s}+e^{i\phi_i}e^{it_iH_s})$, so
$\norm{B_i-C_i}\le\frac12(\norm{\tilde S_i(t_i)-e^{-it_iH_s}}+\norm{\tilde S_i(-t_i)-e^{it_iH_s}})\le\epsilon_i$,
where $\epsilon_i$ is the product-formula bound (symmetric in the sign of $t$) plus the synthesis error
of the pulse's rotations. Since $\norm{B_i},\norm{C_i}\le1$, telescoping gives
$\norm{\prod B_i-\prod C_i}\le\sum_i\epsilon_i$. A synthesized trial circuit prepares $\ket{\tilde\psi}$
with $\norm{\tilde\psi-\psi}\le R_{\rm trial}\delta$, and $\norm{\prod B_i}\le1$, so the total error of the
unnormalized output is at most $\epsilon_U$.
\end{proof}

With $\tilde v=\prod B_i\ket{\tilde\psi}$, $\norm{\tilde v-v}\le\epsilon_U$. The distance from $v$ to the line
through $\tilde v$ is at most $\norm{v-\tilde v}$, so $\sin\angle(v,\tilde v)\le\epsilon_U/\norm v\le
\epsilon_U/\sqrt{p_g}$. The triangle inequality for the trace distance gives the bound on $D$. The
states $\hat v$, $\ket g$ and the normalized output are pure, so $D^2=1-|\langle g|\tilde\psi_{\rm out}\rangle|^2$;
since $\ket g$ lies in the ground space, $\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi_0\tilde\rho)\ge|\langle g|\tilde\psi_{\rm out}\rangle|^2$,
which gives \eqref{eq:cert}. (For a mixed output, e.g.\ under noise, a trace-distance bound alone would
not bound the ground-space weight by $D^2$; this case is outside the theorem.) The
success probability is $\norm{\tilde v}^2\ge(\norm v-\epsilon_U)^2\ge(\sqrt{p_g}-\epsilon_U)^2$. For
the energy, the normalized state has weight at least $1-D^2$ on the ground space and at most $D^2$ on
levels $\le E_{\rm top}$, so $\langle H\rangle-E_0\le(E_{\rm top}-E_0)D^2$. \qed

\section{Dyadic versus optimized SBC, and cost maps}
\label{app:dyaopt}\label{sec:dyaopt}

The two SBC schedules reach the same physics by different routes. The dyadic ladder has zero phases, so it
passes the ground state with $f(E_0)=1$ and its length grows in whole pulses. The optimized design
(times and phases from SBC's recipe with a certified suppression constraint) is shorter, at the price of
$f(E_0)<1$: its measured success probability is a median \optPgMed{} times $\gamma$ (as low as
\optPgLo). Measured per shot, the optimized filter uses a median \odShotTime{} times the dyadic evolution
time and \odShotSteps{} times its Trotter steps; its tolerated step is the same, median $\delta
t=\fDtOTwo$ at $\epsilon=10^{-2}$ and \fDtOFour{} at $10^{-4}$. Per success, after the lower success
probability is paid, it is still a median \odMMed$\times$ the dyadic cost (range \odMLo--\odMHi) and the
cheaper one in \odMWins{} of \goNcases{} cases. Certified, the advantage is larger, a median \odCMed$\times$
(cheaper in \odCWins{} of \goNcases), because the optimized filter's leakage bound is nearly tight while the
closed-form dyadic bound is loose (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}), and the shorter filter needs a looser Trotter
budget; its certified cost lies \goCertRatioOptMin--\goCertRatioOptMax$\times$ (median
\goCertRatioOptMed) above the measured one, against \goCertRatioMed$\times$ for dyadic.

Counting the cheapest of the three filters case by case, the optimized SBC filter wins \bestOpTwo{} of
\goCasesTwo{} cases at $\epsilon=10^{-2}$ (dyadic the other \bestDyTwo), \bestOpThree{} at $10^{-3}$
(dyadic \bestDyThree, QETU \bestQeThree), and \bestOpFour{} at $10^{-4}$, where QETU takes \bestQeFour{} and
dyadic \bestDyFour. The cost laws of Table~\ref{tab:gofit} make the trade-off explicit: the optimized
measured cost grows more steeply with the gap ($\Delta^{-\goMOgap}$ against $\Delta^{-\goMDgap}$), because its
design is matched to the gap, and less steeply with $L$. The price of the optimized schedule is its
design: a nonconvex optimization of about 40\,s per case that converged to a filter with $f(E_0)^2\sim10^{-3}$
in \optRetried{} of \optPoints{} measured points and had to be repeated with other seeds; the dyadic ladder
needs no design and never fails.

\emph{Cost maps.}

Two maps make the interplay visible. At fixed $N$ and $\epsilon=10^{-2}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:gridmap}), the
contours of the dyadic cost per success run nearly parallel to the gap axis for $g\gtrsim0.40$: there the
overlap alone sets the cost, from $2$--$6\times10^3$ CNOTs at $\gamma=0.9$ to $1$--$4\times10^5$ at
$\gamma=0.03$; below $g\approx0.40$ the gap starts to matter, and $g=0.30$ costs 1.3--2.7 times $g=0.40$ at the
same overlap. The optimized filter is cheaper over most of the map, and its contours tilt toward the gap axis
over the whole range, as its steeper gap exponent implies. Per shot (not shown) the contours of both turn by
ninety degrees and the gap alone sets the circuit. At fixed gap and overlap (Fig.~\ref{fig:heat}), the
measured cost per success grows 7--21$\times$ (dyadic) from $\epsilon=10^{-2}$ to $10^{-5}$ and
1.8--4.6$\times$ from $N=8$ to 14; the contours tilt toward the $\epsilon$ axis, so over this range the target matters more
than the size. The optimized filter's lead is largest at loose targets and at $\gamma=0.5$, and shrinks
toward $\epsilon=10^{-5}$.

\section{What the Chebyshev filter needs}
\label{app:empirical}\label{sec:empirical}

To see how the gap and the overlap act on the Chebyshev filter itself, we replace the certificate
by simulation for $N=8$--16 (\empN{} cases). Each trial state is decomposed on the exact eigenbasis
(dense diagonalization per $S^z$ block), so the smallest QETU degree $d_{\min}$ that reaches $\epsilon$ for
that state is found exactly; at that degree we then find the smallest number of Strang steps per
query with the Trotterized circuit simulated on the full state vector, allowing a slightly higher
degree when it makes one step per query enough. Three families separate the inputs. In the first,
$J_2=0.2411$ for every $N$ and the gap closes with $N$. In the second, $J_2$ is chosen at each $N$ (by
bisection on ED) so that the gap is $E_1-E_0=0.40$ exactly ($J_2=\empMatchJ$ for $N=8$--16). In the
third, $J_2$ is tuned at $N=10$, 12 and 14 to gaps from 0.30 to 0.55. For the trial states we use the
optimal MPS at $\chi=1$, 2, 4 and synthetic states $\sqrt{\gamma}|E_0\rangle+\sqrt{1-\gamma}|\xi\rangle$ with
$\gamma$ from 0.03 to 0.9, $\xi$ being the normalized excited part of the $\chi=2$ MPS, so that the overlap
changes while the shape of the excited weight does not. $\epsilon\in\{10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4}\}$.

\emph{The degree is set by the gap and the target alone.} The measured degrees collapse onto
\begin{equation}
d_{\min}\,\Delta\;\simeq\;\frac{2}{\pi}\cosh^{-1}\frac1\eta,\qquad \eta^2=\frac{\gamma\epsilon}{1-\gamma},
\label{eq:dmin}
\end{equation}
for every family, trial and target (Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical}b). This is the certified degree with the
true gap: the certificate asks for a median \empDegRatio{} times $d_{\min}$. Where the excited weight
of the trial sits does not help. The Chebyshev filter is equiripple, so every state above the gap is
suppressed by the same factor $1/T_d(1/x_s)$, and a trial whose excited weight lies far above the gap
needs the same degree as one whose weight sits at the gap. Over the gap scan $d_{\min}\propto\Delta^{-\empGapDeg}$.

\emph{The certificate's Trotter count is pessimistic, not its filter.} One Strang step per query was
enough in all \empKone{} cases, against a certified median of \empCertK{} steps per query. Commutator
bounds are worst-case over all states, but the filter only has to act well on a state supported near
the bottom of the spectrum. As a result the certified cost is a median \empCertRatio{} times what the
filter needs (range \empCertRatioMin--$\empCertRatioMax$), and the real cost follows the degree:
\begin{equation}
\text{cost}_{\rm real}\;\approx\;\frac{c_{\rm step}(N)}{\gamma}\,d_{\min}
\;\propto\;\frac{c_{\rm step}(N)\,W}{g\,\gamma}\,\ln\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma\epsilon},
\label{eq:costreal}
\end{equation}
with $c_{\rm step}$ the CNOTs of one step ($\propto N$) and $g=E_1-E_0$. Fitting the simulations gives
overlap $\gamma^{-\empGamLog}$ once the logarithm is divided out ($\gamma^{-\empGamRaw}$ as a plain power,
against $\gamma^{-\empGamCert}$ for the certified plan) and gap $\Delta^{-\empGapExp}$ (certified
$\Delta^{-\empGapCert}$). With the gap held at 0.40 the cost grows as $N^{\empNMatch}$; at fixed $J_2$,
where $g$ also closes, as $N^{\empNFixed}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:empirical}c). The difference is the
$1/g$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:costreal}.

\emph{Gap and overlap are not independent inputs.} Tuning $J_2$ to hold the gap moves the chain toward
the dimerized point $J_2=1/2$, where a $\chi=2$ MPS is close to exact: at the gap-matched couplings the
$\chi=2$ overlap is 0.97--1.00 at every $N$, against 0.70--0.90 at $J_2=0.2411$. Up to $J_2\approx1/2$ a larger gap and a better
trial come together, so for real trials the two effects compound; beyond it the gap keeps
growing while the $\chi=2$ overlap falls again (to 0.78 at $J_2=0.68$, $N=14$). Holding one of them fixed
through $J_2$ therefore always moves the other, which is why the scans here use synthetic trials and
Sec.~\ref{sec:gapov} a sublattice rotation of a real trial circuit.

\section{Reproducing the results}
\label{app:repro}

The code, raw results and paper sources are at \url{https://github.com/aaronseymour7/FilterPipeline}. After
\texttt{pip install -r requirements.txt}, all commands below run from the repository root.
\texttt{python sbpipe\_study/run\_study.py all} runs the DMRG front end (cached), the validation (A),
scaling (B), sensitivity (C), ansatz (D) and oracle (E) studies and writes \texttt{results/*.json};
\texttt{python sbpipe\_study/make\_figures.py} writes every figure, table and the numerical macros used
in this text. The stored results were produced with per-term CNOT counts and converted to the bond compile of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:bond} by \texttt{sbpipe\_study/recompile\_results.py}, which checks every stored count against the
per-term formula before replacing it; a fresh run counts bonds directly. \texttt{python -m pytest sbpipe/tests} runs the component checks (Pauli algebra, Weyl
bound, Trotter bounds against dense matrix exponentials, the Dirichlet identity~\eqref{eq:dirichlet},
filter certificates against a dense grid, gate counts against an explicit merged sequence and against
qiskit, and an end-to-end certificate check).
